The brief
The requirements, restated


A schema is a set of promises. Before looking at any table, here are the twenty business rules those promises have to keep. The last section maps each one back to the column or constraint that keeps it.


The twenty rules


    

  1. Departments have a name, and no two departments share a name.
    2. 

  2. Every department has a description; it is never left blank.
    3. 

  3. Every employee has a name, a work email, a job title, a date joined and a salary.
    4. 

  4. Email identifies a person: no two employees share one.
    5. 

  5. An employee belongs to at most one department.
    6. 

  6. Most employees have a manager who is another employee. The top of the tree has none.
    7. 

  7. Every employee has exactly one of three roles: employee, manager or HR admin.
    8. 

  8. Employment status is one of active, on leave or terminated.
    9. 

  9. Leave types are data, not code: annual, sick and unpaid to start, and HR can add more.
    10. 

  10. Each employee has one allowance per leave type per year, with a count of days already used.
    11. 

  11. A leave request covers a date range and carries a reason.
    12. 

  12. A request moves draft, then submitted, then approved or rejected.
    13. 

  13. A request can be cancelled at any point before a decision.
    14. 

  14. Approving a request increases the employee's used count for that type and year.
    15. 

  15. Nobody is approved past their remaining days.
    16. 

  16. No two live requests for the same employee cover the same dates.
    17. 

  17. Every decision records who made it.
    18. 

  18. Only the employee's own manager or an HR admin may decide a request.
    19. 

  19. Employees see themselves; managers also see their reports; HR admins see everyone.
    20. 

  20. Salary is visible only to the person, their manager and HR admins.
    21. 



🧭
Where rules 18 to 20 live. The last three rules are not in the schema at all. The API enforces them on every request, per caller, and the schema's only job is to make the check cheap: manager_id is the whole answer to "is this person my report". Rules 15 and 16 are similar in spirit: the schema stores the numbers and dates, and the application does the comparison.

The whole picture
The map: six tables


Everything hangs off employees. employee_credentials holds each person's login hash in a table of its own, departments describe where a person sits, leave_types is the short list of kinds of leave, and the two leave tables record what a person is allowed and what they have asked for.


Entity relationship diagram




Reading key. The thick teal line between employee_credentials and employees is the only one-to-one in the schema: a bar at both ends, because each side has at most one row for the other. Every other solid line is a foreign key: the 1 end is the table being pointed at, the ∞ end is the table holding the foreign key column (the "many" end). The dashed line is the second link between the same two tables, and the loop on the right of employees is the table pointing at itself. leave_types is pointed at by both leave tables, so it carries two lines.

Table 1 of 6
departments


The smallest table, and the first one an employee row points at.


Columns


ColumnTypeNull?Constraints and default
id PKINTEGER—PRIMARY KEY
department_nameTEXTNOT NULLUNIQUE
descriptionTEXTNOT NULLa department always says what it does


Why these constraints


department_name is UNIQUE because rule 1 says two departments cannot share a name, and a rule that matters belongs in the database, where no code path can forget it. Without it, two requests creating "Engineering" at the same moment would both succeed.


description is NOT NULL by decision: every department states what it does, so a department cannot be created half-described. The cost is that a creator who has nothing to say must still write something.


💡
Three columns is enough to earn a table. The alternative is a department name repeated on 400 employee rows, with no single place to rename it. Here a rename is one UPDATE on one row, and every employee follows.

employees


The centre of the model: people, their role, their place in a department and their place in the reporting tree.


Columns

No credentials in this table. There is no password column here, on purpose. SELECT * FROM employees can no longer expose a hash, because no hash lives in the table, and an employee who has no row in employee_credentials simply has no login yet. The hash has its own table, documented at the end of this section.


work_email: unique, and so already indexed


Uniqueness is the rule (rule 4). In SQLite a UNIQUE constraint builds an index behind the scenes, so the login lookup WHERE work_email = :work_email is already fast and the schema adds no second index on it. The indexes it does add are on department_id and manager_id, the two columns you filter by constantly.


Why department_id is nullable


Someone can be between teams, and a department you want to delete should not force you to delete the people in it. A nullable foreign key lets the row stay valid with "no department" while the rest of the row is untouched. Contrast that with manager_id, which is also nullable but for a structural reason covered in the next section.


role and emp_status are stored as text


Both columns hold the lowercase string value ('hr_admin', 'on_leave'), not an integer code and not the uppercase enum member name. A row reads correctly in a GUI or in sqlite3 output without a join or a lookup table, and the API serialises the same string straight to JSON, so the React client can switch on it.


Salary is a whole number


💰
Never store money in a float. Binary floating point cannot represent 0.1 exactly, so sums and comparisons drift by fractions of a cent and totals stop reconciling. salary is an INTEGER of whole currency units, which is exact, sums cleanly and sorts correctly. If you ever need cents, store cents as an integer, never a float.


employee_credentials: the login, in its own table


ColumnTypeNull?Constraints and default
employee_id PK FKINTEGER—PRIMARY KEY, and FOREIGN KEY → employees.id ON DELETE CASCADE
password_hashTEXTNOT NULLthe stored hash, never the password


The interesting column is the first one. employee_id is both the primary key and a foreign key, so the row is identified by the employee it belongs to, and there is no separate id at all. A primary key cannot repeat, which means a second credential row for the same employee is impossible. That is the whole one-to-one, enforced by the database; the Which side holds the foreign key section below explains why it works.


No row means no login yet. A newly added employee has an HR record and nothing in this table until someone sets a password. When an employee row is deleted, ON DELETE CASCADE deletes their credential row with it, so a hash is never left behind for a person who no longer exists. The cascade only fires when foreign keys are switched on for the connection, covered in the SQLite section.

One table, two roles
The self-reference: a manager is an employee


A manager is not a separate kind of record. It is an employee whose id appears in someone else's manager_id.


A foreign key to its own table


manager_id points at employees.id in the same table. Arjun's row has manager_id = NULL, and that NULL is how you find the root of the tree: the person nobody manages. Priya's row has manager_id = 2, Arjun's id. A reporting line is a tree you walk by following the column, one row at a time. The recursive query that walks the whole subtree at any depth is query 2b in Seven questions, eight queries.


What the foreign key does not stop


The constraint stops you pointing at an employee who does not exist. It does not stop a cycle: A manages B and B manages A satisfies every foreign key, and each row on its own looks fine. Nothing in the schema can say "this graph must be a tree". That is a gap the application has to check, by walking up from the proposed manager before saving and refusing if it meets the employee being edited.


Also not stopped
An employee can be their own manager (manager_id = id), which is the shortest possible cycle. The same application check catches it.

leave_balances columns


One row is one allowance: this person, this leave type, this year.

The composite unique is the heart of the page


UNIQUE (employee_id, leave_type_id, consumed_year_in) is what makes "Priya's annual allowance for 2026" exactly one row. The database refuses a second row for the same triple, so no code path can create a duplicate that later disagrees with the first. entitled_days starts as a copy of leave_types.days; copying it means a balance already issued keeps its number even if the type's default changes later.


The usual first attempt


The common mistake is to hang an annual_days column directly off employees. It fails three ways: a single column cannot hold a different number for sick and annual; it cannot hold last year's number next to this year's; and it has to be edited in place every January, destroying the history it overwrites.


First attempt: columns on employees
employees
  id, name, ...,
  annual_days_left   -- one number, which year?
  sick_days_left     -- a new column per type
  -- last year? gone, overwritten in January
This design: one row per allowance
leave_balances
  employee_id | leave_type_id | consumed_year_in | entitled | used
  3           | 1 (annual)    | 2026             | 20       | 4
  3           | 2 (sick)      | 2026             | 10       | 1
  3           | 1 (annual)    | 2025             | 20       | 18


🏦
Think of a bank statement, not a balance on a card
A row per employee, type and year is a statement per period. Last year's statement stays in the drawer; this year's starts fresh. Nothing is overwritten, and a new leave type is a new row in leave_types, not a schema change.

Table 6 of 6
leave_requests


One row is one ask for time off, and it names the person who decided it.


Columns


ColumnTypeNull?Constraints and default
id PKINTEGER—PRIMARY KEY
employee_id FKINTEGERNOT NULLFK → employees.id, indexed
leave_type_id FKINTEGERNOT NULLFK → leave_types.id
start_dateDATENOT NULL—
end_dateDATENOT NULL—
reasonTEXTNOT NULLevery request states a reason
no_of_daysINTEGERNOT NULLdays charged, fixed when the request is made
approver_id FKINTEGERNULLFK → employees.id; unset until someone decides
request_statusTEXTNOT NULLindexed; one of draft / submitted / approved / rejected / cancelled, checked by the application


A date range, not a row per day


A request is stored as start_date and end_date, both plain DATE values: leave is taken in whole days, so there is no time of day to store. A row per day would turn a two-week holiday into ten rows that must all be approved, cancelled or rejected together, and a request would no longer be one thing you can point at. The range stays one row, and the overlap test in the queries section works directly on the two dates.


no_of_days is stored on the row


The number is decided when the request is made. Weekend and holiday rules change later, and if the count were recomputed from the dates every time, an old request would silently change its cost. A stored number keeps old requests truthful.


approver_id is empty until someone decides


approver_id is NULL while the request is a draft or waiting, and holds the deciding employee's id afterwards. It is a second foreign key to employees, the dashed line on the diagram. The table keeps no timestamp for the decision: the schema records who decided, not when, and ordering by id is the stand-in for "oldest first".


🔗
There is no foreign key to leave_balances. The pair (employee_id, leave_type_id) plus the year taken from start_date locates the balance row. That is deliberate and it has a cost; see Decisions and trade-offs.







8
One column, five values
The status lifecycle


leave_requests.request_status holds one of five values, and only some moves between them are legal.


Legal transitions




  1. The employee creates a draft and edits it freely.
  2. They submit it, and it now waits on their manager.
  3. The manager or an HR admin approves (used days go up) or rejects.
  4. Before any decision, the employee may cancel.


Only the current status is stored


The schema keeps the current status and nothing else. If a request goes draft, submitted, approved, the earlier states are not recoverable, and the table has no timestamps to say when anything happened. If you needed that, you would add an append-only leave_request_events table with one row per transition. This design does not build it.

Your own two questions
Which side holds the foreign key


Two questions come up in every first schema. Here are the direct answers, and the answer to the second one is the best idea on this page.


Does the "many" table always hold the foreign key?


Yes. For a one-to-many relationship, the foreign key goes on the many side, always. The alternative fails: to put it on the "one" side, a single cell would have to hold a list of ids, and a relational column holds exactly one value. Walking the seven one-to-many foreign keys on this page (the eighth, on employee_credentials, is a one-to-one and gets its own section just below):


RelationshipReads asMany sideForeign key


departments to employeesone department has many employeesemployeesemployees.department_id


employees to employeesone manager has many reportsemployees (the reports)employees.manager_id


employees to leave_balancesone employee has many allowancesleave_balancesleave_balances.employee_id


leave_types to leave_balancesone leave type has many allowancesleave_balancesleave_balances.leave_type_id


employees to leave_requestsone employee has many requestsleave_requestsleave_requests.employee_id


leave_types to leave_requestsone leave type has many requestsleave_requestsleave_requests.leave_type_id


employees to leave_requestsone approver has decided many requestsleave_requestsleave_requests.approver_id




The junction table


For many-to-many, the joining table is called a junction table. Join table, bridge table and associative table are the same thing under different names. It turns one many-to-many into two one-to-manys, with each foreign key on the junction side.


✨
leave_balances is a textbook junction table between employees and leave_types. An employee has many leave types, and a leave type applies to many employees. leave_balances sits between them holding one foreign key to each, which is exactly the two one-to-manys a junction produces. And it carries its own data, entitled_days, used_days and consumed_year_in, which belong to neither side alone: they describe this person, with this type, in this year. A junction that carries its own columns is the usual sign you were right to make one, and it is why the composite unique over the two foreign keys plus the year is the page's key constraint.


The one-to-one: the foreign key is the primary key


The page now has all three shapes, and they differ in where the foreign key lives. employee_credentials is the one-to-one: its employee_id is the foreign key and the primary key. A plain foreign key allows many rows to point at the same employee; a primary key allows only one row per value. Make the same column both, and the primary key constraint is exactly what forbids a second credential row for an employee.


🔑
Three shapes, decided by where the foreign key sits.
One-to-one: the foreign key IS the primary key (employee_credentials.employee_id). One row per employee, and uniqueness comes from the key itself.
One-to-many: the foreign key sits on the many side as an ordinary column (leave_requests.employee_id). The same employee id can repeat down the column.
Many-to-many: a junction table holds two foreign keys, made unique together (leave_balances). Each pair appears once, while either side can appear many times.
Same mechanism every time, a foreign key. What changes is whether that column is also the primary key, an ordinary column, or half of a composite.


Are leave requests and employees many-to-many?


Not directly. A leave request belongs to exactly one employee, so employee to request is one-to-many and employee_id lives on leave_requests. Ask in both directions: one employee, many requests; one request, one employee.


But leave_requests is itself a junction in two other relationships, because it holds two more foreign keys:


    

  • Employees to leave types. Through leave_type_id, an employee takes many types and a type is taken by many employees. This is the same pair that leave_balances joins, but here each row is an event (a dated request), not a standing allowance.
    • 

  • Employees to employees, twice. Directly, manager_id is a one-to-many tree inside employees. Through leave_requests, employee_id (the requester) and approver_id (the decider) are both employees, so many requesters are linked to many approvers with each request as the link.
    • 



Industry practice
Ask the cardinality question in both directions
For any two tables A and B, ask "how many B can one A have?" and then "how many A can one B have?". One and one is a one-to-one: merge the tables, or give the second table a foreign key that is also its primary key, as employee_credentials does. One and many is a one-to-many: the foreign key goes on the many side. Many and many is a many-to-many: add a junction table. Two quick questions replace most of the guesswork.

What happens on delete


Apart from the one cascade on employee_credentials, the schema leaves ON DELETE at its default, NO ACTION. Deleting a department that still has employees, a manager who still has reports, or a leave type that any balance or request uses, fails at the foreign key. For departments and managers the application clears the children first by setting their column to NULL. A leave type in use should simply never be deleted. The alternative for the nullable columns is ON DELETE SET NULL, letting the database do the nulling itself. Credentials are the opposite case: a hash has no meaning without its employee, so CASCADE is the right rule there.

Does the design answer real questions?
Eight questions, ten queries


A schema is good if the questions people actually ask are short queries. Named parameters (:like_this) are placeholders for values the caller supplies. Because leave types are rows now, every query that names a type joins to leave_types and filters on lt.type instead of comparing a string stored on the row.


1. Everyone in a department


query 1
SELECT id, name, job_title
FROM employees
WHERE department_id = :department_id
ORDER BY name;


Uses ix_employees_department_id.


2. Reports of a manager


Direct reports first:


query 2a
SELECT id, name
FROM employees
WHERE manager_id = :manager_id;


Uses ix_employees_manager_id. Then the whole subtree at any depth, using a recursive common table expression. The depth < 20 guard stops the walk if a cycle ever got in (see the self-reference).


query 2b
WITH RECURSIVE subtree(id, name, manager_id, depth) AS (
    SELECT id, name, manager_id, 0
    FROM employees
    WHERE id = :root_id
  UNION ALL
    SELECT e.id, e.name, e.manager_id, s.depth + 1
    FROM employees e
    JOIN subtree s ON e.manager_id = s.id
    WHERE s.depth < 20
)
SELECT id, name, depth
FROM subtree
WHERE depth > 0
ORDER BY depth, name;


Uses the primary key to start and the manager_id index at each level.


3. Remaining annual leave this year


query 3
SELECT lb.entitled_days - lb.used_days AS remaining
FROM leave_balances lb
JOIN leave_types lt ON lt.id = lb.leave_type_id
WHERE lb.employee_id = :employee_id
  AND lt.type = 'annual'
  AND lb.consumed_year_in = CAST(strftime('%Y', 'now') AS INTEGER);


Joins leave_balances to leave_types so the question can say "annual" rather than a number. Uses the automatic index behind the composite unique, which begins with employee_id.


4. Requests waiting on a manager


query 4
SELECT r.id, e.name, lt.type, r.start_date, r.end_date
FROM leave_requests r
JOIN employees e ON e.id = r.employee_id
JOIN leave_types lt ON lt.id = r.leave_type_id
WHERE r.request_status = 'submitted'
  AND e.manager_id = :manager_id
ORDER BY r.id;


Uses the request_status index to find submitted rows, then the primary key to reach each employee and leave type. With no timestamp column, id orders the queue oldest first.


5. Who is on approved leave on a given day


query 5
SELECT e.id, e.name, lt.type
FROM leave_requests r
JOIN employees e ON e.id = r.employee_id
JOIN leave_types lt ON lt.id = r.leave_type_id
WHERE r.request_status = 'approved'
  AND r.start_date <= :day
  AND r.end_date >= :day;


Uses the request_status index; the date comparison then filters those rows.


6. One employee's leave history


query 6
SELECT r.id, lt.type, r.start_date, r.end_date, r.no_of_days, r.request_status
FROM leave_requests r
JOIN leave_types lt ON lt.id = r.leave_type_id
WHERE r.employee_id = :employee_id
ORDER BY r.start_date DESC;


Uses the employee_id index.


7. Total sick days company-wide last month


query 7
SELECT COALESCE(SUM(r.no_of_days), 0) AS sick_days
FROM leave_requests r
JOIN leave_types lt ON lt.id = r.leave_type_id
WHERE lt.type = 'sick'
  AND r.request_status = 'approved'
  AND r.start_date >= date('now', 'start of month', '-1 month')
  AND r.start_date <  date('now', 'start of month');


Uses the request_status index. A request that straddles two months is counted in the month it starts; that is a reporting choice, not a schema limit.


8. The overlap check a new request must pass


overlap check
SELECT 1
FROM leave_requests
WHERE employee_id = :employee_id
  AND request_status IN ('submitted', 'approved')
  AND start_date <= :new_end
  AND end_date   >= :new_start
LIMIT 1;


Any returned row means the new request collides with a live one, whatever its leave type, so this query needs no join. It uses the employee_id index.


Why this predicate is right
Two date ranges overlap unless one ends before the other starts. So they do not overlap when existing.end < new.start or new.end < existing.start. Negate that with De Morgan and you get existing.start <= new.end AND existing.end >= new.start. Note the inclusive <= and >=: a request ending on Friday and another starting that same Friday do overlap. Most people write this one wrong by testing whether the new range's endpoints fall inside the old one, which misses the case where the new range swallows the old.


9. Logging in: the credential lookup


Fetch the person and their hash in one trip, by work email:


query 9a
SELECT e.id, e.name, e.role, e.emp_status, c.password_hash
FROM employees e
JOIN employee_credentials c ON c.employee_id = e.id
WHERE e.work_email = :work_email;


Login is now a join, which is the price of the split. The plain JOIN is the point: it returns zero rows for an unknown email and for an employee who has no credential row. A missing credential row must fail login exactly like a wrong password: same outcome, same message, so a caller cannot tell "no such account" from "no password set" from "wrong password". Do not use a LEFT JOIN here and then treat a NULL hash as a special case; that special case is the old sentinel coming back.


The same table answers the HR question the other way round, who cannot log in yet:


query 9b
SELECT e.id, e.name, e.work_email
FROM employees e
LEFT JOIN employee_credentials c ON c.employee_id = e.id
WHERE c.employee_id IS NULL
ORDER BY e.id;


Here the LEFT JOIN is wanted: it keeps employees with no match, and the IS NULL on the credential's key keeps only those.

Every choice has a price
Decisions and trade-offs


Each decision below is stated as the choice, what it costs, and what you would do instead.


used_days is stored, not summed


Choice: keep a running used_days on the balance row. Gain: fast reads and a one-column remaining-days check. Cost: the number can drift out of step with the requests it summarises. Alternative: sum approved no_of_days on demand, which can never drift but costs a query per read. The atomic conditional UPDATE in Part 2 chapter 8 is what keeps the stored number honest.


no_of_days is stored on the request


Choice: save the count at request time. Cost: a number that could disagree with the dates if edited carelessly. Alternative: compute from the dates every time, at the price of old requests changing when the rules do.


No foreign key from requests to balances


Choice: locate the balance by (employee_id, leave_type_id) plus the year of start_date. Gain: a request can be drafted before its balance row exists, and the two tables stay independent. Cost: the database cannot tell you that a request has no matching balance, and a request spanning New Year has to pick one year. Alternative: a balance_id column on the request.


Leave types as a lookup table, not an enum


Choice: leave types live in leave_types, and both leave tables hold a leave_type_id foreign key. You chose this over an enum.

-- adding a type is data, not a deploy
INSERT INTO leave_types (type, days)
VALUES ('parental', 90);

-- the database refuses a type that
-- is not a row (foreign key)
Enum in a column
-- values fixed in the application
leave_type  'annual' | 'sick' | 'unpaid'

-- a new type needs a code change,
-- a release, and sometimes a migration


For the lookup table: adding a type is an INSERT by an HR admin with no release; the default allowance lives next to the name; a typo like 'anual' cannot get in because the foreign key rejects an id that does not exist; and the type can be renamed in one place. For the enum: the code and the data cannot disagree, because the code is the list; there is no join, so balance and request queries are shorter and cheaper; and a type that the application knows about at compile time can be handled specially without a lookup. The cost you accepted: the database can no longer constrain the value itself, only that it is some row of leave_types, so a rule like "unpaid never has a balance" is application logic; and every balance and request query that names a type needs a JOIN to leave_types, as every query above shows. Alternative: keep a text column with a CHECK (leave_type IN (...)), which gets database enforcement back but is a schema change to extend.


Enum values as text


Choice: lowercase strings in role, emp_status and request_status. Leave type is not in this list any more; it became a table, see the next decision. Cost: no database-level check that the value is one of the allowed set; the application enforces it. Alternative: a CHECK constraint or a lookup table with a foreign key.


terminated is a status, not a delete


Choice: keep the row and set emp_status = 'terminated'. Why: requests, decisions and balances still point at that person, and deleting the row would either fail at the foreign key or orphan history. Cost: every "active people" query must filter on the status.


Weekends are the application's problem


Choice: the schema stores a count; the application decides which days count. Why: weekend and holiday rules are policy, and policy changes faster than table definitions.


One department per employee


Choice: a nullable department_id on the employee. Cost: nobody can belong to two departments. Alternative: a junction table employee_departments, which is correct the day the first person genuinely sits in two.


Credentials in their own table


Choice: the password hash lives in employee_credentials, one row per employee, not as a column on employees. Why:


    

  • Different lifecycles. The HR record exists from the day someone is hired. A login exists only once a password is set, and changes, expires or is reset on its own schedule.
    • 

  • No sentinel. With the hash on employees the column had to be NOT NULL, so an employee who had never logged in needed a made-up value such as '!unset' in it. A sentinel is evidence that a column is in the wrong place. Now "no login yet" is simply no row.
    • 

  • No leak by accident. SELECT * FROM employees can no longer return a hash, whoever writes it and wherever the result ends up.
    • 

  • Room to grow. If you later add single sign-on or API keys, this table can become one-to-many without touching the HR record.
    • 



Cost: login becomes a join (query 9a), and there is a second table to create and delete in step. The honest counter-argument: for a strict one-to-one, many engineers call the split premature, since a one-to-one is often just one table drawn twice. Django and Rails both keep the hash on the user model. But those tables are user tables: identity and login are the same thing there. Here employees is an HR record that holds salary, a manager and a department, and the login is a separate concern that happens to attach to it. Alternative: a nullable password_hash column on employees, simpler to read and to query, with the leak risk and the NULL special case coming back.

The engine under the schema
What SQLite does differently


SQLite is a database in a single file. It is excellent for a tutorial and a few surprises follow from that.


Types are hints, not rules


SQLite uses dynamic type affinity. A VARCHAR(100) does not enforce 100 characters; the length lives in the application. There is also no native DATE, DATETIME or BOOLEAN type: dates are stored as ISO-8601 text ('2026-03-15'), which sorts and compares correctly as a string. That is why the overlap check can use plain <= on dates.


INTEGER PRIMARY KEY is the rowid


A column declared exactly INTEGER PRIMARY KEY is an alias for SQLite's internal row id and fills itself in with the next number when you insert without one.


Foreign keys are off until you turn them on


⚠️
Foreign keys are not enforced unless you set PRAGMA foreign_keys=ON on every connection. Without it, every REFERENCES on this page is documentation only. Part 1 chapter 5 turns it on for every connection.


This now has a concrete consequence. ON DELETE CASCADE on employee_credentials only fires when PRAGMA foreign_keys=ON is set on the connection doing the delete. On a connection where it is off, deleting an employee leaves their credential row behind: a password hash that belongs to nobody, and, if the id is ever reused, to the wrong person. The pragma is not an optimisation; with the cascade in place, it is correctness.


WAL, and one writer at a time


WAL mode lets a GUI read the file while the server writes it. SQLite still allows one writer at a time, and the read-then-write race on a leave balance is what makes the fix in Part 2 chapter 8 worth teaching.


ALTER TABLE is limited


ALTER TABLE can add a column but cannot drop or retype one. A real change to an existing column is a table rebuild: create the new table, copy the rows, drop the old one, rename.


What Postgres gives you instead
Real types and real concurrency
Postgres has genuine DATE, TIMESTAMPTZ, BOOLEAN and enum types, and enforces VARCHAR(100). It has a daterange column type with an exclusion constraint, which would enforce the no-overlap rule (rule 16) inside the database instead of in a query the application must remember to run. SELECT ... FOR UPDATE locks a row for the length of a transaction, and writers run truly concurrently.

Honest boundaries
What this design deliberately does not do


Each gap below is a conscious omission, with the shape of its fix.


leave_types.days has no year on it


A leave_types table now exists, so leave kinds and their default allowance are data. The gap that remains is that days is a single global number: annual is 20, for everyone, with no year attached. Change it to 22 next January and the default for 2026 is overwritten, so last year's entitlement can no longer be read from the types table, and nothing lets the allowance differ by office or by seniority either. Balances already issued keep their own entitled_days, which is why the history is not wiped from them, but the rule that produced those numbers is gone. The shape of a fix: key the allowance by year, either a year column on a policy table or a separate leave_policies table with employees assigned to a policy, and generate each January's balances from it. It would sit beside the six tables, not change them.


One credential per employee


Because employee_id is the primary key of employee_credentials, there is still only one credential per employee: one password hash, nothing else. Single sign-on or API keys would break that. The fix is to turn this one-to-one into a one-to-many auth_identities table, with its own id, the employee foreign key as an ordinary column, and a column saying what kind of identity it is. Existing hashes would move across as rows of kind "password".


The rest


    

  • No public holidays table. The application cannot know a holiday that is not in its code.
    • 

  • No history of changes to title, salary or department. "What was Priya's title last June" is unanswerable.
    • 

  • No audit log and no timestamps. Who changed what, and when, is not recorded; the rows carry no created or decided time.
    • 

  • No half-days. no_of_days is an integer.
    • 

  • No attachments. A sick note has nowhere to live.
    • 

  • One company, one currency, one timezone. No tenant column, no currency code.
    •

Takeaways
    

  • Six tables: departments, employees, employee_credentials, leave_types, leave_balances, leave_requests.
    • 

  • A one-to-one makes the foreign key the primary key (employee_credentials); a one-to-many puts the foreign key on the many side; a many-to-many needs a junction table.
    • 

  • Credentials live apart from the HR record: no row means no login yet, and a missing row fails login like a wrong password.
    • 

  • For one-to-many, the foreign key always lives on the many side.
    • 

  • leave_balances is the junction between employees and leave types, carrying entitled_days, used_days and consumed_year_in; its composite unique is the page's key idea.
  • Leave types are rows, so adding one is an INSERT; the price is a JOIN in every query that names a type.
    • 

  • A self-referencing foreign key models a tree but cannot forbid a cycle.
    • 

  • Store money as integers, dates as ISO text (DATE columns) in SQLite, and turn foreign keys on per connection.
    • 

  • Whatever the schema cannot enforce, the application must; know which rules are which.
